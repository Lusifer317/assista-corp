Guide 01 · Executive support

How to scope executive assistant support

Executive assistant support works best when a business defines the recurring tasks, the executive's preferences and the decisions that must remain with the executive. A job title alone is not an operating brief.

1. Start with a task inventory

List the recurring activities that consume leadership time: calendar coordination, meeting preparation, inbox triage, travel research, action tracking, document organization or CRM administration. For each task, record how often it occurs, who requests it, what information is needed and what a completed result looks like.

2. Separate preparation from approval

Write down what an assistant may complete independently, what requires review and what must always be escalated. For example, an assistant may prepare a draft response or shortlist travel options, while the executive retains approval of sensitive messages, expenses and commitments. Do not share personal passwords; provision named user access with only the permissions needed.

3. Document the working rhythm

Agree priority rules, working hours, time zones, preferred channels, meeting cadence, urgent-contact routes and backup expectations. Capture these in a short SOP and keep it current as responsibilities change.

4. Review useful operating measures

Track practical indicators such as overdue action items, scheduling rework, meeting-preparation completeness and unresolved requests. Set definitions and targets with the team after observing a baseline; do not assume a generic productivity percentage applies to every executive.

Guide 02 · Finance operations

Building a reviewable reconciliation workflow

A reconciliation process should make it clear which records were compared, which items matched, what remains unresolved and who approved the final accounting action. Administrative support can help prepare and organize that work, but it should not blur financial authority.

1. Define sources and cadence

List each source statement, transaction export, ledger or supporting record, its owner and the period it covers. Agree file naming, cut-off times, reporting cadence and the person responsible for resolving missing source data.

2. Make matching rules explicit

Document the fields used to match records, such as date, amount, reference or counterparty. Define how partial matches, duplicates, reversals, foreign-currency items and timing differences should be flagged. Keep source references so a reviewer can reproduce the result.

3. Maintain an exception register

For each unmatched item, record a stable reference, amount, source, reason category, date identified, assigned owner, status and required evidence. Escalate aged or material exceptions according to thresholds set by the client's finance owner.

4. Separate preparation and authorization

Use role-based access and a reviewer/approver model. Payment release, movement of funds, journal approval, tax positions, statutory filings and financial sign-off should remain with the client's authorized personnel and professional advisers. Avoid sending bank credentials through inquiry forms or ordinary email.

Guide 03 · Customer experience

Designing a practical customer support queue

A support queue becomes easier to manage when incoming requests are classified consistently, routed to clear owners and escalated using documented rules. The aim is reliable handling—not a single response-time promise detached from workload or coverage.

1. Categorize the reasons customers contact you

Begin with real contact reasons from your inbox, ticketing system or chat history. Create categories that help agents route work, such as order status, account questions, product information, returns, technical issues or complaints. Avoid building an overly complex taxonomy that agents cannot apply consistently.

2. Define priority and escalation

Set clear criteria for urgent, sensitive or business-critical cases. Document who owns safety concerns, privacy requests, payment disputes, account-security issues, policy exceptions and high-value customer escalations. A support representative should not improvise policy decisions or promise an exception without authorization.

3. Build an approved knowledge base

Maintain current product facts, policies, step-by-step resolution guidance and response templates. Assign an owner and review date to each knowledge article. When a policy changes, update the source material and notify the people using it.

4. Measure quality as well as speed

Review response accuracy, policy adherence, documentation quality, repeat contacts, backlog age and escalation outcomes. Segment results by channel and coverage window so the data reflects the actual operating model.

Guide 04 · Recruitment operations

Creating a consistent recruitment coordination process

Recruitment coordination is easier to manage when every role begins with an agreed brief, screening evidence is captured consistently and the hiring team retains ownership of selection decisions.

1. Align on the role before sourcing

Record the role purpose, required skills, seniority, location, working model, compensation range, essential criteria and interview stages. Identify which criteria are mandatory and which can be developed after hiring.

2. Use a structured screening rubric

Build a role-specific scorecard based on observable evidence. Use the same criteria for comparable candidates and record where evidence came from. Screening administration can organize information and flag gaps, but it should not replace contextual recruiter review or become an automated decision about a person's suitability.

3. Maintain clear candidate records

Keep stage, source, contact history, interview availability and next action current in the approved applicant tracking system. Limit access to people who need it, follow the client's retention rules and avoid copying candidate personal data into unapproved spreadsheets or messaging channels.

4. Keep decision ownership with the hiring team

Recruiters and hiring managers retain responsibility for interviews, selection, compensation, offers, employment terms and background-check decisions. A coordinator can keep scheduling and feedback moving without taking over those decisions.
